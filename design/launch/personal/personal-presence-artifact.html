Part 0

The rules everything below was checked against

Derived from design/07-EXPERIENCE-BIBLE.md Part VIII and the current (2026-08-25) rewrite of COPY.md — shorter and wittier than the pack before it. Don't revert toward the older, more formal register.

1. No exclamation marks. No emoji. Nowhere.

2. The institution never says "we/our/us" — third person only for TimelyCapsule's own voice. This does not apply to Assad's personal posts: a real person saying "I" is correct, not a violation.

3. Dates literal and absolute; relative time may accompany, never replace.

4. Never "just." State, never sell.

5. No manufactured urgency, no fake traction, funding, customers, press or testimonials. No engagement-bait questions. No hashtag spam.

6. Vocabulary: seal/sealed, record, deliver, travelled/remaining, kept, recipient, the wait — never save/lock, post/content, send/push, progress/ETA, stored, user, the countdown.

7. Banned outright: "excited to announce," "game changer," "revolutionary," "disrupting," "the future of," "journey," "thrilled," "humbled," and "Founder | CEO | Entrepreneur | Startup Guy | Building in Public" as a headline formula.

Platform limits verified per string, not assumed: X bio ≤160, X post ≤280, Instagram bio ≤150, GitHub bio ≤160, LinkedIn headline ≤220, About ≤2600 (only ~300 chars show before "see more" — front-loaded below), LinkedIn post ≤3000 (kept to 900–1300).

Eight considered. The goal stated for this pack: Assad → software engineer / builder → building TimelyCapsule, not Assad → personal brand → marketing TimelyCapsule.

A"Founder, TimelyCapsule"

credible very generic obvious TC link poor brand fit

The exact energy being avoided — indistinguishable from a thousand other headlines. TimelyCapsule's own voice is dry and specific; this is a title card, not a voice.

B"Building TimelyCapsule"

medium credible moderately generic strong TC link decent fit

Active and humble, but alone doesn't establish what Assad does. "Building @X" is itself a common bio pattern now — better than "Founder," not distinctive.

C"Software engineer building TimelyCapsule"

most credible structure is common clear TC link good fit

Leads with craft, not authority — closest of the eight to the existing real GitHub bio ("Software Engineer."). Backup recommendation.

D"Founder / Builder, TimelyCapsule"

hedges between two framingsitself a cliché shape now

Not recommended.

E"TimelyCapsule — letters that arrive later"

n/a credibility distinctive TC-only, no Assad wrong direction of relationship

Doesn't say what Assad does — a company tagline worn as a personal headline. This is the "personal brand is the product" failure mode explicitly being avoided. Good as a line inside an About section; not as the identity itself.

F"Software engineer. Currently: TimelyCapsule."

credible not generic clear TC link best brand fit

Borrows the brand's own "shipping manifest" register (07 §VIII: "plain, exact, unhurried") without quoting it — distinctive because it sounds like a fact being stated. Primary recommendation.

G"Engineer. Time, mostly."

too vaguefails "obvious enough to find"

Clever without being useful — a stranger has no route to TimelyCapsule from this alone.

H"Building things that wait. Currently: TimelyCapsule."

thematically tighttakes a beat to parse cold

Strong as an About-section opening line, once TimelyCapsule has already been named once beneath it. Not strong enough alone.

Part 4

Nine LinkedIn company posts

Six pair with an existing real-UI asset; three are text-only — LinkedIn handles pure text natively, and not every post needs a prop. Suggested order is the numbering below. Cadence: 1–2/week, slower than a personal or product social account — matches the brand's own "unhurried" register.

01 · Why this existsx/post-mark.png
Most software wants you back tomorrow. A notification, a streak, a reason to open the app again before you forget it exists. TimelyCapsule is built to be forgotten. You write something, choose a date — a month out, or 2041 — and from that point the product's only job is to do absolutely nothing until that date arrives. No nudge. No "come back and see." The waiting itself doesn't need you. That's a strange thing to optimize a product for. It also seems like the only honest way to build something that promises to hold a letter for ten years.
02 · What I actually care aboutscreenshots/06-survival.png
The part I actually care about: I can't read your letters. Every capsule is encrypted on the device that writes it, before any of it reaches a server. What arrives at TimelyCapsule's database is ciphertext — no plaintext copy exists anywhere else, not in a log, not in a backup, not in a support tool. Practically: there's no "reset your password and we'll help recover your data" flow the way most products have one. Lose your key, and the content is unrecoverable — including by me. Not a caveat. The actual design.
03 · Nothing is happeninginstagram/12-waiting.png
What "nothing is happening" actually looks like. This is a real screenshot of a letter, three years into a ten-year wait. No progress bar animating toward a goal, no "you're 30% of the way there" — just two numbers, travelled and remaining, and a date. The test I hold this screen to: if a capsule sitting untouched for three years reads as empty instead of kept, the design has failed. Most software that shows you "nothing is happening" is broken. This one is working exactly as intended.
04 · The refusalsinstagram/07-refusals.png
Refusing the growth-team playbook, on purpose. There's a well-understood toolkit for making people open an app more often: streaks, badges, "on this day" memories, progress bars, push notifications timed for re-engagement. None of it is in TimelyCapsule, and none of it is going to be. A sealed letter isn't content to be surfaced back to you — it's something you asked to be left alone until a specific date. Nudging you before then would be a small, quiet betrayal of the one promise the product makes.
05 · What belongs insideinstagram/11-sealing.png
What belongs inside a letter, besides words. A letter in TimelyCapsule can carry more than text: a photo, the coordinates of where it was written, or a voice note — recorded, played back, and only enclosed once you've actually decided to keep it. Nothing attaches automatically the moment you stop recording; you hear it back first. The voice note is the one I think about most. A photo preserves what something looked like. A voice note preserves how someone actually sounded saying a thing — cadence, the pause, the parts a transcript would flatten out. A different, harder kind of keeping.
06 · Not the last wordinstagram/14-replies.png
The letter doesn't have to be the last word. I didn't plan to build a reply feature. But every early conversation about this product hit the same question: what if they want to write back? A capsule's recipient can now reply — encrypted the identical way the original letter was, so the answer is exactly as unreadable to me as the letter that prompted it. Not a message thread. No read receipt, no typing indicator, no notification with a content preview. A second letter, answering the first, on its own schedule.
07 · Built to destroy somethinginstagram/15-one-reading-only.png
A feature whose entire job is to destroy something. Most products treat "delete" as an edge case. TimelyCapsule has a mode called "one reading only" where destruction is the entire point: the moment it's opened and read, the record is gone. Not archived. Not soft-deleted with a recovery window. Gone — no copy anywhere, including in any backup. Longer than I'd like to admit went into making sure that fires only after a confirmed read — not a page load, a prefetch, or a link-preview crawler. That needed a dedicated test suite before I trusted it enough to ship.
08 · A bug about a bugtext only
A bug that only existed because the fix for a different bug was too good. Shipped a fix recently so a logged-in user's dark-mode preference would stick properly on the marketing pages. Verified it worked. Moved on. Two days later: every hard refresh of a logged-in page was bouncing real, authenticated users straight back to the login screen — a session with valid tokens sitting right there, ignored. The cause: a loading-state variable that used to safely default to "false" (not loading) now needed a third state — "haven't checked yet" — and defaulting it to false meant the route guard concluded "not loading, no user, redirect" before the code that reads the tokens ever ran. False and unknown had been silently treated as the same value. Fixed by giving it three real states instead of two. A very small diff for how long it took to notice.
09 · Honest statusscreenshots/05-hero.png
Where this actually is, honestly. TimelyCapsule is live at timelycapsule.com. Anyone can write a letter and seal it right now, with or without an account — a guest capsule needs neither, just a link to hand to whoever it's for. It's one person building it. No funding round to announce, no growth numbers worth quoting yet, nothing to disrupt. Just a product that does one specific thing — hold a letter until a date you choose — and refuses to do much else.

7.1LinkedIn — ten

TC L1 · What most of the actual work is
Somewhere around the third rewrite of the encryption flow, I realized the interesting problem was never "how do I build a time capsule app." It was "how do I build something where the correct answer to almost every feature request is no." No read receipts. No "seen by." No preview of what's inside before it opens. No way for me — the person who runs the servers — to see what anyone wrote. Every one of those is a feature I chose not to build, on purpose, more than once. That's most of what I actually do most days now — not adding things, holding a line on what doesn't get added. Stranger kind of engineering work than I expected. I like it more than I thought I would.
TC L2 · Shipping replies
Shipped replies to TimelyCapsule this week — a capsule's recipient can now write back, sealed the same way the original letter was. The interesting part wasn't the crypto (mostly reused work I'd already done). It was resisting the urge to make it feel like every other reply feature I've built before: no read receipt, no typing indicator, no notification with a content preview. It just says someone replied. You still have to go open it to find out what they said — same as the letter that started it. Staying consistent with a product's own principles is more work than building the feature.
TC L3 · Testing a delete button
Wrote a whole test suite this week for a feature whose only job is to delete something. TimelyCapsule has a "one reading only" mode — read it once, and the record is destroyed, no copy anywhere. The hard part wasn't the deletion logic. It was making sure it could never fire by accident: not on a page load, not on a link-preview bot crawling the URL before a real person opens it, not on a browser prefetch. Confirmed-read only, verified against an actual test suite — not eyeballed once and shipped. Destructive features earn a different level of paranoia than the rest of the codebase.
non-TC L4 · The dependency I never audited
Spent an embarrassing amount of time this week convinced a race condition was in my own code. It was in a library I'd imported for one utility function two years ago and never looked at again past the README. "I only need one function from this" is exactly the reasoning that gets you here. Auditing dependencies you only "need one function from" is one of those lessons that doesn't stay learned — I'll be back here again in six months, differently annoyed.
non-TC L5 · Building alone
Nobody warns you that building alone means you're also QA, support, and the person who has to explain to yourself why a decision from four months ago made sense at the time. Started keeping a written log of decisions as I make them — not for anyone else, just so future-me has something better to work from than "I'm sure I had a reason." Small habit. Has already saved me from re-litigating two decisions I'd already made correctly once.
adjacent L6 · Making a decade feel true
A decade is 3,653 days. Written down like that it barely registers — small enough that a calendar app could show you a countdown without embarrassing itself. Most of what I've actually worked on this year has been trying to make that distance feel true inside an interface, instead of just numerically correct. Two numbers — travelled, remaining — turned out to do more of that work than any animation would have.
non-TC L7 · Tests that lie
The tests I trust least are the ones I wrote in a hurry to make CI green, then never looked at again. Found three of those this week, all subtly wrong in the same direction — asserting the mock's behavior instead of the real one's. None of them were catching anything. All three had been green for months. A passing test suite is a claim, not a proof, and I'd stopped checking which one I actually had.
non-TC L8 · The four-word code review
The best code review comment I got this year was four words: "why does this exist." Not unkind. Just correct. I didn't have a good answer, so I deleted forty lines instead of defending them. Most of the code I'm least proud of has survived exactly because nobody asked that question at the right moment.
TC L9 · A bug that only existed because a fix was too good
Shipped a fix recently so a logged-in user's dark-mode preference would stick properly on the marketing pages. Verified it worked. Moved on. Two days later: every hard refresh of a logged-in page was bouncing real, authenticated users straight back to the login screen — a session with valid tokens sitting right there, ignored. The cause: a loading-state variable that used to safely default to "false" (not loading) now needed a third state — "haven't checked yet" — and defaulting it to false meant the route guard concluded "not loading, no user, redirect" before the code that reads the tokens ever ran. False and unknown had been silently treated as the same value. Fixed by giving it three real states instead of two. A very small diff for how long it took to notice.
non-TC L10 · The Windows 95 site
My personal site is themed like Windows 95. It has taken noticeably longer to maintain than it has any right to, for a decision that gains me nothing measurable — nobody has ever hired me because of it, as far as I know. I have no plans to make it sensible. Some projects are for the portfolio. That one's just for me.
