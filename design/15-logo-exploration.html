Meridian V2 · Phase 10 · Exploration

Logo & brand mark

Six directions, three rejected with reasons, one developed into a full system — and one inherited decision overturned because two approved documents contradict each other.

Before drawing · the constraints already decided

What the earlier work has already ruled out

These are not preferences. Each is a decision already taken and signed off, and together they eliminate most of what a logo could be.

ConstraintSourceWhat it removes
Behave, don't depict04 · 07 §II.3Hourglass, vault, clock face, capsule, envelope, seal, tree, key
The mark never animates05Any idea whose value depends on movement
Brass means waiting and nothing else05The accent colour is unavailable to the logo. The mark is achromatic — see below

Must work in both registers05 · 06Anything relying on a single ground or a fill effect
Engraves and embosses05Gradients, soft edges, varying stroke weight, fine detail
Holds at 16px05Anything with more than ~3 discrete elements

The colour constraint is stronger than it looks, and I want to state it plainly: the logo cannot be brass.

05 reserves brass for waiting — “the moment it decorates a heading, the reserved meaning is gone.” A logo is the most-repeated element in any identity, so putting brass in it would destroy the reservation faster than anything else could.

So the mark is ink on ground, or ground on paper. Achromatic, permanently. That is unusual and it is an advantage: it is what institutions actually do — a hallmark, a watermark, a seal die, a banknote intaglio. None of them are coloured. It also means the mark survives fax, engraving, embossing, single-colour print and a monochrome favicon without a variant.

Part II · Critique

Why three were cut, and why the inherited recommendation was overturned

A · The Meridian — rejected as primary, and this overturns 05.

Two problems. First, a vertical line crossed by a horizontal one is a plus sign, a dagger, or a crosshair. At 16px it is indistinguishable from a “+” button. There is no ownable character in it — it is the least differentiated form in the entire exploration, which is fatal for the one element whose whole job is recognition.

Second, and more seriously: it depicts a crossing that has already happened. 03 §VII is explicit that the mark must hold “tension, not completion — something approaching a line, not resting on it. A moment just short of happening, held still.” A graduation sitting on the meridian is the moment after. It encodes arrival, and arrival is the one thing the brand is not about.

05 and 03 contradict each other here. 05 nominated the Meridian (ii) as the symbol and demoted the Transit (i) to “if one is wanted later.” That choice was made on engraving and legibility grounds, which are real, but it did not test the mark against 03 §VII's requirement. Resolving in favour of 03, because it is the more specific instruction about what the mark must carry.

C · The Vernier — rejected on scalability, exactly as predicted.

05 abandoned the Dial Segment for failing below 24px. The Vernier fails the same way for the same reason: five discrete elements at different weights. At 16px the short ticks merge into the baseline and it reads as a signal-strength icon or a barcode. It is the handsomest of the six at 96px and unusable at 16px, and the small size is the one that matters — it is the favicon, the app icon at its smallest, and the embossed blind stamp.

D · The Seal — rejected on principle, and it would have been rejected on craft anyway.

It depicts. A broken ring is a picture of a seal, and 07 §II.3 forbids illustrating our own metaphors: “the moment we illustrate our metaphor we have admitted the interface isn't carrying it.”

Independently, a ring is the single most generic mark in software. And the break — the thing that carries all the meaning — is the first detail to disappear at small sizes, leaving a plain circle. A mark whose meaning is the first casualty of scaling is not a mark.

E · The Registers — rejected, reluctantly.

Conceptually the richest of the six: it carries the two-register system, the bounded record and the meridian in one figure. But three overlapping forms at three weights is roughly twice the complexity budget a 16px mark allows, and the filled block reads as a solid rectangle the moment it goes below 24px, at which point the whole idea collapses.

It also fails the behave, don't depict test on a technicality that turns out to matter: it is a picture of our own interface. That is a diagram, not a mark.

F · The Interval — held as runner-up, not developed.

The strongest idea in the set. The product is the gap between two moments, and a mark whose subject is the negative space is conceptually exact. It engraves superbly and is unmistakably not a software logo.

Two reasons it is not the recommendation. It is symmetrical, so it has no direction — no sense of approach, no before and after, and therefore none of the tension 03 §VII requires. And at small sizes two vertical bars with a rule between them read as a “pause” glyph, which is an unfortunate thing for a product about waiting to be mistaken for.

Kept on file. If the Transit ever proves too slight, this is where to go next.

Part III · The recommendation

B · The Transit, as a three-density system

A marker approaching the meridian and not reaching it. Two elements, one relationship, and the relationship is the brand: something is on its way to a fixed appointed moment.

The scalability answer is not to simplify the mark until it survives 16px — it is to let the mark shed detail at defined thresholds, the way a chart sheds gridlines. Three densities, one idea.

A correction I only found by rendering it. The graduations first ran across the meridian, projecting equally either side. At 96px that read as a double dagger — reintroducing the exact crossbar problem Direction A was rejected for, in the mark meant to replace it.

They now project left only, into the side the marker travels through. The meridian stays an uninterrupted vertical, and the ticks read as a scale the marker is moving along rather than as something crossing it. Nothing may cross that line except at transit.

The one change that makes it survive 16px: the ring fills. An open ring at 16px becomes a grey smudge — the counter closes up and the stroke and hole merge. Filling it keeps a hard, readable silhouette.

This is a meaning change, not just a drawing change, and it happens to be the right one. An open marker is in transit. A filled marker has weight — it is the same distinction the product draws between a capsule pending and a capsule sealed. At sizes too small to read nuance, the mark states the simpler fact.

Why the gap is 22 units and not zero

The marker sits at x=22, the meridian at x=44 — the marker has travelled exactly half the distance it will travel. Not nearly-arrived, which would read as impatience; not barely-started, which would read as inert. Held at the midpoint, the mark is permanently in the middle of a wait, which is where a capsule spends 99% of its life (03 §IV).

Part IV · Both registers

Ink on ground, ground on paper

The same geometry, no variants. The mark is achromatic in both registers, so there is no “logo on dark” and “logo on light” file — there is one mark and it inverts.

Engraved and embossed

A blind emboss carries no ink at all — only a light and a shadow edge. This is the hardest test any mark faces, and it is the one the Transit is best suited to: two forms, uniform stroke, no counters below 2mm.

Part V · App icon & favicon

The tile

The icon is ground, not paper. An app icon sits on a home screen among competitors, and the instrument register is where our chrome lives — a paper tile would read as a document app. The mark is centred on the optical axis rather than the geometric one, because the meridian's weight sits right of centre.

Part VIII · The problem I cannot design around

The wordmark commits you to the name

09 §XIV.1 recorded this as the largest unaddressed risk to the recognisability goal, and it is still open: “TimelyCapsule” is a SaaS name attached to a grave, institutional, horological identity. Timely means punctual or opportune — mildly ironic for a product about long waits — and the compound reads like project-management software.

Everything in Parts I–V is rename-proof. The Transit is a geometric mark with no letterforms in it; it survives any name you choose. Part VI does not: a wordmark is the one deliverable that hard-codes the decision, and it is also the piece that goes on every invoice, email footer and app store listing.

Recommendation: approve the symbol now, hold the wordmark. The symbol unblocks the favicon, the app icon, the embossing die and the screen rebuild — everything the next phase actually needs. The wordmark costs a day whenever the name is settled, and redoing it later costs far more than that.

Related and still open from 09 §XIV.2: Meridian is currently both the brand and the design system. If Meridian became the product name, the mark above needs no change at all — which is worth knowing before deciding.

Part IX · What I would still test

Honest gaps

  • The 16px favicon has not been tested in a real browser tab against a strip of other favicons. That is the only test that matters for recognition at that size, and a rendered mock-up flatters it.
  • The ring may be too slight at physical small sizes. On a debossed business card at 8mm, a 0.4mm stroke can disappear into the stock. A test emboss would settle it; my instinct is the stroke wants to be ~15% heavier for physical production than for screen.
  • Directional bias is untested. The marker approaches from the left, which reads correctly in left-to-right scripts. In Arabic or Hebrew contexts the mark may read as departing rather than approaching. 05 already limits us to Latin-plus, so this is not blocking — but it is worth knowing before any RTL launch.
  • Direction F should be drawn properly before final sign-off, not just sketched. It scored 41 on a first pass and I developed the leader instead; if you dislike the Transit, F is the one to develop, not A.
