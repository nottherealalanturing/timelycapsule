Meridian V2 · Phase 10 · Exploration II

Six conceptual families

The first exploration was six variations pretending to be six alternatives. This one starts from six different ideas about what the company is, and judges the results as logos before judging them as symbols.

The correction

What was wrong with the first round

Every one of the six directions was built from the same two primitives: a vertical rule, and a small element beside or upon it. Meridian, Transit, Vernier, Registers and Interval are all the same drawing with different spacing. Only the Seal sat outside that family, and it was rejected in a sentence.

So the comparison was rigged. A scorecard across six siblings measures which sibling is tallest, not whether the family is right.

And the winning argument was philosophical, not visual. Transit won on “tension, not completion” and “position, not progress” — both real requirements, both invisible to anyone who has not read 03.

Judged only as a logo, a small circle beside a vertical line is two of the most generic primitives in existence. It is a bullet point next to a rule. It is a slider handle. It is the record button in a video app. Simple is not the same as distinctive, and I conflated the two because the idea behind it was good.

The test used below is the one you set: would this be memorable to someone who knows nothing about us? A mark earns its meaning afterwards. It has to survive the first two seconds on its form alone.

Family I · Transit

Motion toward a fixed moment

I

Transit

Something is on its way to an appointed moment. The mark is a reading taken mid-journey — a marker approaching a reference it has not yet reached.

Succeeds as a symbol, fails as a logo. Inside the product it is superb — it is literally the waiting screen and the Gate 1 reveal geometry, so the chrome and the mark are one idea. Outside the product it is a dot and a line, and it carries no memory. Nobody describes it to a friend.

Demoted, not discarded. See Part VIII.

Demoted to motif
Family II · Institution

The thing that holds

II

The Keep

We are not a journey, we are a custodian. The mark is an enclosure with something inside it: brackets, a cartouche, an assay mark. The visual language of registries, hallmarks and banknotes rather than of software.

The strongest logo in either exploration. It is a containing form, which is what almost every durable mark has in common — a silhouette you can hold in your head. It reads instantly and without any briefing: something is being held, and held carefully.

It also says the brand's one word. 02 settled on Kept — kept time, kept safe, kept its word. This is that word drawn.

II·c is cut: a hexagon with a bar is a hazard placard or a crypto token, and the six vertices mush below 24px.

II·d is cut, reluctantly, and it was the interesting one. Replacing the right bracket with a bare meridian fuses this family with Transit: custody on the left, the appointed moment on the right, the marker between them. Conceptually it is the most complete mark in either exploration. Drawn, it reads as a bracket pair whose right half is broken — an error rather than an intention — and the marker crowds the meridian. A mark that looks like a mistake cannot be the mark.

Leading candidate
Family III · Correspondence

A letter, franked

III

The Postmark

The product is a letter that arrives late. The visual language is the cancellation: a datestamp ring, killer bars, the mark a post office strikes to say this passed through here, on this date.

The most instantly readable of the six, and the most dangerous. Everyone knows a postmark, which is the strength. But a ring with a bar through it is also the universal “no” sign — prohibition, blocked, forbidden. At small sizes III·a is a no-entry glyph, and no amount of brand-building overrides a symbol that literal.

III·b avoids that but is two objects rather than one silhouette, and the killer bars read as a menu icon or a signal meter.

It also depicts — a picture of a postmark on a product that forbids illustrating its own metaphors (07 §II.3).

Rejected
Family IV · Keepsake

The object, contained

IV

The Vessel

What we hold is a physical thing in all but fact. The mark is nested containment — a form inside a form — or a join: two halves that meet and stay met.

Rejected on collision, not on craft. A filled square inside an outlined square is already the stop button in every media player, the “copy” icon in every toolbar, and the selection indicator in every design tool. It is one of the most occupied forms in software.

IV·b is more distinctive but reads as a plumbing fitting or a Tetris piece, and its meaning is opaque even with the briefing — which is the failure mode we are explicitly trying to avoid.

Rejected
Family V · Legacy

Accumulation across generations

V

The Strata

Time deposits layers. Growth rings, sediment, the accumulating distance between who wrote and who reads. The mark is not an instant but a stack of them.

Rejected, decisively, at 16px. Horizontal bars of varying length are a hamburger menu, a text-align icon, or a signal meter. There is no size at which V·a is not one of those things.

V·b is genuinely handsome at large sizes and is the only mark here that suggests accumulated duration rather than a single instant. But concentric arcs are radar, wifi, a loading indicator or a sound wave — and “reads as loading” is a constitutional failure, not a matter of taste (07 §VI.3).

Rejected
Family VI · Threshold

The moment of opening

VI

The Aperture

The brand's single most emotional instant is the seal giving way. The mark is a boundary that has just parted — an opening, a gap in something previously whole.

The closest challenger to The Keep, and it loses on one detail. VI·a is structurally the same containing form — but parted, and therefore empty. It says the thing has already gone.

That is the Direction A error again in another costume: it depicts the moment after. The brand lives in the years before, and an empty enclosure is a picture of a wait that is over.

VI·b reads as a coin slot, a piggy bank, or a ballot box.

Rejected

Part VIII · The recommendation

II·a — The Keep, marker centred

Two brackets and a marker. The brackets are a custody: something is inside them, and they are closed on it. It is the brand's one word — Kept — as a drawing.

I changed this recommendation after rendering it, and the change matters. I first proposed the marker off-centre, to smuggle the Transit idea — position, part-way through — into a form that works as a logo.

Drawn at real sizes, the offset is invisible. Set beside the centred version it does not read as a position; it reads as a printing error. It was a meaning only I could perceive, which is precisely the failure you called out in the first exploration — philosophy doing work the form is not doing.

So it is centred. A symmetrical, confident enclosure is the better mark, and the anticipation it gives up is carried by the Transit motif inside the product, where there is room to say it properly.

Why it beats Transit on your own test

  • It is a shape, not an arrangement. You can draw it from memory after one look, and what you draw is unmistakably it. A dot beside a line, redrawn from memory, is just a dot beside a line.
  • It means something before it is explained. Enclosure reads as protection in every culture that has ever made a container. Transit means nothing until someone tells you the line is a date.
  • It is the brand's one word. 02 settled on Kept. This is that word as a drawing — and the word came first, in a document written long before any mark.
  • It is symmetrical, which is why it holds still. 07 §II.2 requires the institution to never change while the instruments always do. A balanced enclosure is a fundamentally static form; the moving parts live in the product, not the mark.
  • Nothing in software looks like it. Brackets-with-contents is typographic territory, not iconographic; it has been left alone because it is not a picture of anything.

The honest weakness. Brackets are close to a text-selection or code idiom, and there is a real chance a developer audience reads them as syntax rather than custody. It is also the least emotional of the finalists — it says safe more loudly than it says waiting, and the off-centre marker is doing a lot of quiet work to fix that.

If that trade is wrong, the fallback is not Transit — it is VI·a Aperture with the marker restored inside it, which I would draw next.

Sizes

The only change across the ladder is stroke weight: the brackets thicken as the mark shrinks so the counter between bracket and marker stays open. Nothing is dropped, because there is nothing to drop — which is what a mark of three elements buys you.

Part IX · What happens to Transit

Demoted, and better for it

Transit stays — as the product's geometry, not as the logo. It is already the waiting screen, the countdown axis and the Gate 1 reveal: a marker approaching a meridian. That is a motif, and motifs are supposed to live inside a product and repeat.

Asking it to also be the identity was the mistake. A motif can be quiet and abstract because it accumulates meaning over hundreds of exposures inside the app. A logo gets two seconds on a home screen next to forty competitors and has to work cold.

So: The Keep is the mark. The Transit is the motif. They share a language — an enclosure and a marker within it — so the app icon and the waiting screen are visibly the same family without being the same drawing.

Part X · Still open

What I have not settled

  • The wordmark still commits you to the name. Unchanged from the first exploration and unchanged by this one — 09 §XIV.1 remains open. The Keep is rename-proof; the lockup is not.
  • The bracket-as-syntax risk is untested. Worth showing II·b to five people who have never heard of the product and asking only “what is this?” If two say “code”, the offset marker needs to grow or the brackets need to lose their square corners.
  • Bracket weight for physical emboss — same open question as before; my instinct is ~15% heavier than screen.
  • I have not drawn a monogram. A seventh family — letterform-derived — is the one territory still unexplored, and it is the most common solution for institutions. I left it out because it hard-codes the name, and the name is unresolved. If the name settles, it should be drawn before final sign-off.
